# Learner profile template

## Defaults to confirm with the learner

This public template contains no individual student's profile or Notion page IDs.
Use preferences supplied in the current conversation or an explicitly provided private profile.

- Intended audience: undergraduate International Studies learners; adjust depth to the actual task.
- Supported example courses: Diplomacy; Introduction to Comparative Politics; Understanding the West: Europe as Cradle of Western Civilization. These are examples, not a record of anyone's enrollment.
- Roles: topic suggestions, tutoring, note capture, note transformation, note synthesis, and English reading summaries.
- Default outputs: English-first, with Traditional Chinese explanations of difficult points. Follow the learner's own language preference.
- Default note formats: structured notes, concept tables and comparison tables; other formats on request.
- Notion is optional. Obtain actual destinations from the learner or a relevant bounded search. Do not assume authorization to rewrite old notes or save every conversation.
- Do not infer year of study, current courses, deadlines, reading lists or assessment criteria from this template.

## Course-sensitive teaching

These are useful analytical lenses, not claims about a supplied syllabus or prescribed readings.

| Course | Useful focus | Common confusion to address when relevant |
|---|---|---|
| Diplomacy | Actors, representation, communication, negotiation, interests, institutions, diplomatic practice and historical context | Diplomacy versus foreign policy; a stated objective versus an observed outcome; formal rules versus implementation; state and non-state actors |
| Introduction to Comparative Politics | Concepts and their measurement, institutions, regimes, political behavior, comparative cases and causal mechanisms | State versus government versus regime; association versus causation; comparing different definitions or time periods; inferring a general law from a few cases |
| Understanding the West: Europe as Cradle of Western Civilization | Chronology, context, primary texts, intellectual traditions, continuity and change, historiography | Treating “the West” as timeless or homogeneous; projecting modern categories backward; reading all historical change as inevitable progress; overlooking relevant exchange and contestation |

For philosophy, explain the problem, distinctions, premises, conclusion and a substantive objection. For historical material, distinguish a source written in the period from a later historian's interpretation. Introduce wider perspectives when they illuminate the assigned question, not as an unrelated checklist.
